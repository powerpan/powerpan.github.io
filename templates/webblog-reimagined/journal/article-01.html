The Props Are Fake: On Good Trip

The props are fake. The tears an actor sheds in front of them are real.

2026.11.086 minNo. 01
IntroductionThe Word XiangAfter Sammo Hung Turns UpRememberingOkinawa, One More TimeCoda

A daughter keeps watch at a hospital bedside. Suddenly the film crew calls: could she fetch a big soup bowl from the prop room?

That's the little errand that starts Good Trip. Shan works in a film art department, going back and forth between the set and the hospital all day. She means to stay with her father, but he insists on coming with her. They slip out of the hospital at night and get the bowl. Then Dad hits the gas and drives to Macau to settle a score with an old friend who once got him into trouble. Next they fly to Okinawa, where he wants to propose all over again to his wife, who runs a travel agency there.

By now you know something is off. How can a man with terminal cancer, who can't even get out of bed, just get up and go?

But the film never meant for you to believe it in the first place.

The Word Xiang

The word that matters most in the Chinese title is xiang.

It can mean wanting to take a trip, or imagining you've already taken it. The director borrows a Tang-dynasty tale, The Disembodied Soul: the body stays in the hospital bed while the soul slips away. The trip feels real and unreal at once. There are period costumes, echoes of old Hong Kong movies, and the absurd moment when Nick Cheung suddenly speaks fluent Japanese.

And the daughter happens to work in film art. Her job is to make things that aren't there look real. Everything in the prop room is fake: fake bowls, fake walls, fake antiques. But a film art assistant knows better than anyone that the props are fake and the tears an actor sheds in front of them are real.

The trip is like that, too. The route is made up, but the wishes her father gets to fulfil and the time the two of them rarely get to spend together are real.

After Sammo Hung Turns Up

Kueng isn't the sort of father who commands respect. He tells terrible food puns, speaks his mind and often embarrasses his daughter. He even combs the few strands on top of his head into a barcode. He keeps saying he'll leave his old car to his children, as though it were some grand inheritance.

He likes to boast, too. His daughter has always taken his stories with a grain of salt.

It's only after her father dies, when she meets Sammo Hung, that she discovers one of his stories really was true.

It's a quiet moment, and one of the heaviest in the film. Her father is gone. He can't prove anything now, and he won't do anything new. But in that second, the father she carries in her head changes. She thought she'd heard it all, thought she had him figured out. There was still a part of him she'd never seen.

Director Norris Wong said something in an interview that explains this scene rather well: "It's not that we don't love each other. We just don't know how to get to know each other."

That's probably as close as it gets to how things are between a lot of parents and children. You know his catchphrases. You know he'll suddenly go quiet when he's angry. You can even guess what he'll say next. But what kind of person was he when he was young? What did he wrestle with before making a particular choice? You don't really know. Even if he told you, you took it for bragging back then.

Remembering

But there's another danger after someone dies.

When we miss someone, it's easy to keep only the good parts. We forgive their faults and polish up their more ridiculous stories. Given enough time, the person in our head becomes gentler, always in the right, perfect. And less and less like the person they were.

The film doesn't do that. The Sammo Hung story being true doesn't make every word Dad ever said believable. And the old car probably isn't worth much. In the end he's still the dad who tells terrible jokes and makes you laugh when you're angry with him. Shan gets to know him again. What she finds isn't a perfect father; it's that she hasn't finished getting to know him.

That moves me more than turning him into a saint ever could.

Okinawa, One More Time

When I read up on the film later, I found there was another daughter outside it.

Norris Wong's father was the only one in the family who supported her making films. Her mother kept urging her to take a civil service exam or become a teacher. After his cancer returned, he still came to the set of My Prince Edward on a walking stick. By post-production he was bedridden, but he still told her to keep making films. He'd also done some work as an extra without her knowing. The last trip they took together was to Okinawa.

As it turns out, there's some of Norris Wong herself in Shan. A daughter who works in film, after losing her father, uses the things she knows best: building sets, finding props, making things that aren't there look real. She puts together a trip for him.

It makes me think the relationship doesn't stop when someone dies. You can't eat or talk together again, and there won't be any new memories you both share. But the person still here goes on living. Perhaps ten years later something happens and you understand why he did what he did. Perhaps someone mentions him one day and you learn he had another side. Or perhaps, like Norris Wong, you put him in a film and let him put on a suit one more time, tell another terrible joke and go back to Okinawa.

That isn't rewriting the past. It's finally being able to see a part of it you didn't understand at the time.

Coda

Dad wanted to travel, but he couldn't go anymore.

So his daughter took that word, xiang, and made a film out of it for him.
