When Data Centers Protect Themselves: A 3.8 GW Load Transfer

The servers stayed powered, but the grid had to absorb a sudden loss of demand. Two investigations offer a different view of protective controls, retries, and system testing.

At 7:56 a.m. on July 22, 2026, a 230 kV transmission line in Northern Virginia suffered a mechanical failure. The protection system cleared the fault, with no relay misoperation or stuck breaker. At the equipment level, that part of the response worked as designed.

The trouble followed. A voltage disturbance caused some data centers to switch to backup power. The sudden reduction in grid demand raised voltage, triggering a second wave of transfers. In less than two minutes, roughly 3,800 MW, or 3.8 GW, of load left the public grid. PJM and Dominion's preliminary investigation identified it as the largest such load transfer PJM had experienced.

This was not 3.8 GW of servers shutting down. Load moved to backup supply; the source of electricity changed. PJM's subsequent event account explicitly says the grid did not experience significant reliability impacts. Frequency reached 60.092 Hz. Operators reduced generation and adjusted reactive power resources; the balancing authority's area control error limit excursion recovered within nine minutes. Dominion reported that customers transferred back to the grid within thirty minutes.

What interests me is the interaction in between. A data center switches supply to keep its business running, but that action changes the electrical conditions other facilities face next. Asking whether the UPS protected the servers is not enough. We also need to ask what happened outside when that load left the grid.

Three disturbances in a minute, then no automatic return

To understand the response, we need to unpack "switching to backup power." A UPS may use batteries to sustain critical loads during a voltage sag and quickly resume drawing from the grid. A diesel rotary UPS using a flywheel and engine may stay on backup longer. Even with the same hardware, an operator's additional control rules can change the recovery behavior. To a server, these all provide uninterrupted power. To the grid, they are not necessarily the same kind of load.

A separate event on July 10, 2024 made this difference concrete. After a lightning arrester failed on a 230 kV line, automatic reclosing produced six successive faults and clearances over 82 seconds. Each lasted 42 to 66 milliseconds and was cleared properly, yet approximately 1,500 MW of data center load left the grid. NERC's incident report documented a typical customer-side rule: count three voltage disturbances within a minute, switch to backup, and stay there until manually reconnected.

The line's reclosing sequence was trying to restore supply. The facility's disturbance counter interpreted repeated abnormalities as a reason not to trust the external source. Around the third voltage depression, about 1,260 MW dropped off and did not return for hours. NERC attributed most of the sustained reduction to the interaction between the reclosing sequence and disturbance-counting controls.

We do not have to assume a UPS broke to explain that sequence. Control rules turned short electrical disturbances into a sustained change in grid demand. But we also cannot use the 2024 "three in a minute" configuration to explain every facility's response in 2026. The latter's public findings remain preliminary, and individual equipment parameters and settings need separate confirmation.

The distinction matters for remediation. If equipment cannot tolerate the disturbance, hardware may need to change. If additional controls move it prematurely into a prolonged backup state, trigger conditions and recovery rules deserve scrutiny. Calling all of it "oversensitive data centers" does not tell us which layer to change.

Why using less power can also trouble the grid

When we discuss data centers and AI, we often ask whether there is enough electricity and how much new capacity GPUs will consume. July's event raised the opposite question: can the grid follow when a large block of demand suddenly stops drawing power?

Generation and consumption must remain balanced. A sudden load reduction before generation adjusts leaves a temporary active-power surplus that pushes frequency upward. Voltage also depends on reactive power balance, line impedance, and control equipment. A voltage rise is not simply another expression of rising frequency. The same load change can trigger both, but their analysis and control differ.

The harder part here was that high voltage after the first load transfer became an input to a second group of protective controls. Not all of the subsequent disturbance came from the original faulted line; some came from the system's response to it. PJM's account records those two waves. Describing it as a single simultaneous "mass shutdown" loses that sequence.

Peak power and projected annual consumption therefore cannot fully describe a large facility's grid impact. Two campuses of the same size can require very different dispatch responses if one resumes demand promptly after fault clearance and the other stays on backup for half an hour. Equal average consumption does not imply equal dynamic behavior.

It also adds a condition to "more redundancy means more reliability." Different facilities can have their own UPS systems, generators, and controllers while remaining exposed to the same voltage disturbance. They need not fail or communicate with one another to take highly correlated actions. More redundant equipment does not automatically remove a shared trigger.

"Local optimum" is a lens here, not a computed result

From an operator's perspective, leaving an abnormal external supply quickly is understandable: keep servers and cooling powered and the business running. The grid's objective is regional active and reactive power balance and frequency stability. A choice that protects one business can increase the public system's control workload.

A "local-optimum trap" captures one aspect of this conflict, but the phrase needs some restraint. Without each facility's objective function, tolerance, costs, and settings, we cannot prove its strategy was mathematically optimal. Still less can we call one disturbance an established Nash equilibrium.

A more defensible interpretation is that these strategies served local reliability goals while their external effects were not sufficiently constrained at system level. The grid adjusted generation and voltage for load that suddenly disappeared. Those costs may not fall entirely on the facility making the transfer decision. There is an externality here, along with a coordination problem between control systems.

That does not mean every independent protective action harms the whole. Small transfers, sufficiently dispersed responses, or a grid prepared for them may make independent protection entirely reasonable. The combination of common triggers, response correlation, scale, and speed needs study. Autonomy itself is not an adequate explanation of failure.

This sequence reminds me of a retry storm

Software engineers know the pattern: a backend slows, clients time out, and they retry. For one caller, another attempt may succeed. For an overloaded backend, thousands of callers trying once more can lengthen the queue and cause still more timeouts.

It is not the same physical process as a power transfer, but the feedback structure is similar. Participants observe a shared environment and take corrective action for their own tasks. Those actions change the environment and prompt further correction. Google's SRE Book discusses retry budgets, randomized backoff, attempt limits, and reducing work under overload.

The budget matters. Suppose each of three layers in a call chain allows three total attempts. In the worst case, the deepest service may receive 27 calls, not three. This is a constructed software example, not data from the grid event. Each layer looks restrained on its own, while their composition can exceed shared capacity.

But "randomized backoff" cannot simply be transplanted into electrical protection. An HTTP call can wait; a dangerous electrical fault may require protective action within milliseconds. Delaying essential protection can damage equipment. The transferable ideas are constraining additional actions, coordinating recovery, and validating collective responses, not copying software timing parameters.

Autoscaling poses a related problem. If several upstream services add instances because their CPUs are busy, but the bottleneck is a shared database, they may merely add more concurrent connections. A test that measures only upstream service throughput can mistake displaced pressure for improvement. Downstream queues, aggregate successes, and recovery time must also be measured.

Testing cannot stop at the UPS output

A facility tests UPS transfers, generator starts, and whether servers stay powered. The grid tests fault clearance, breaker operation, and reclosing timing. Those tests are necessary, but passing on both sides does not establish how the two systems behave together.

Nancy Leveson's STAMP approach to system safety offers a complementary perspective: accidents can arise from unsafe interactions among functioning components, so analysis must address control structures and constraints, not just broken parts. I am not claiming this event has undergone a completed STAMP analysis. The useful reminder is that test boundaries should not stop precisely at organizational or equipment handoffs.

If I were reviewing this interconnection study, I would ask for explicit load-response behavior: which voltage or frequency conditions trigger a transfer, how long they must persist, whether brief disturbances accumulate, whether an off-grid state latches, and who decides to return. A normal power profile cannot explain these state transitions.

Then I would examine many facilities under one disturbance rather than copy a single model hundreds of times and assume independent actions. Similar thresholds exposed to the same voltage trace may cross a boundary together. The grid state changed by the first wave must also feed into the subsequent simulation.

The model itself needs evidence. NERC's March 2026 large-load gap assessment identifies deficiencies in load-model validation and measurement; generator field-test methods cannot simply be reused. A practical path combines simulation, equipment tests, and real disturbance records within explicit safety boundaries, with hardware-in-the-loop validation where appropriate. Demonstrating resilience is not a license to inject faults into a production grid.

I would also review recovery separately. Dispatch may have reduced generation while facilities are off-grid. A large, abrupt reconnection can create an imbalance in the opposite direction. When demand returns, how fast, and with what external coordination deserves testing just as much as the exit. NERC's report on the 2024 event also flags controlled reconnection and ramp rates.

Agree in advance on ride-through and recovery

"Do not switch to backup, for the grid's sake" is too easy an answer. Beyond equipment tolerances, continued grid draw may put servers, cooling, and business operations at risk. Coordination should not abolish protection. It should distinguish disturbances to ride through from conditions allowing a trip, and define recovery afterward.

PJM's September 8, 2026 proposal describes operating regions based on voltage magnitude and duration. In relevant regions, loads may temporarily reduce active power. Once voltage returns above 0.9 and below 1.1 p.u., they must recover to more than 90% of pre-disturbance demand within two seconds. Here p.u. means per unit relative to the nominal value. This is not a blanket requirement to withstand any fault for two seconds.

At the time of writing, the material cited here is a proposal, not a universally effective standard. PJM's September 10 account also discusses transition arrangements with existing facilities. Actual settings must follow applicable requirements, site capabilities, and system studies. Copying a threshold from an article is not a deployment procedure.

Cost allocation matters too. If better ride-through, monitoring, and dynamic modeling mainly reduce public-grid risk while the facility bears the investment alone, technical feasibility may not produce voluntary adoption. Interconnection requirements, responsibilities, and compensation influence that choice. A UPS controller cannot settle all of them.

September 17 offered an instructive contrast. Under supply pressure, PJM activated demand response and received emergency authority to direct data centers and other large customers to backup generation if necessary. Authority does not establish that every customer actually switched. It does show why a transfer is not inherently good or bad: coordinated demand reduction during scarcity and unexpected collective exits during a disturbance have to be evaluated in their respective operating states.

A compiler can reshape the power profile too

There is another route that does not directly concern UPS controls: reducing fluctuations on the compute side. In traditional clusters, unrelated workloads can partly offset one another's power variation. Large synchronized training can make many accelerators compute together and then move together into communication or waiting. Time correlation matters to electrical infrastructure alongside GPU count and total peak power.

In a February 2025 technical article, Google reported cluster-level fluctuations in the tens of MW and described a TPU-compiler intervention. It detects synchronization flags associated with power variation and adjusts activity in major compute blocks around those flags, smoothing utilization over time. In the disclosed test case, fluctuation magnitude fell by nearly 50%, with less than 1% performance impact.

That does not mean electricity bills were halved. The article also reports a small increase in average power. The reduced quantity was fluctuation magnitude, and one case does not establish identical gains for all training jobs. Nor does it directly solve Northern Virginia's protective transfers. It is compute-load shaping, not remediation of voltage-protection settings.

The useful direction, to me, is extending scheduling beyond "keep devices busy" to facility constraints. Job start order, movable batch windows, and campus power ramps may become optimization variables. But synchronized training has dependencies, and shifting computation can add waiting. Measure useful throughput, training time, energy use, and the power profile to decide whether it pays off. GPU utilization alone is insufficient, and business costs cannot be ignored when requesting staggered work.

More capable Agents can still overload shared services

On an Agent platform, the issue can appear in familiar software form. This is a hypothetical scenario, not an observed counterpart to the grid event: an external API slows, so each Agent adds retries, searches, and cross-checks to finish its task. The extra calls lengthen response times, leading the next planning round to decide to "look a little more."

A higher single-task success rate may be bought with more shared resources. If one Agent improves from 95% to 98% while making other tasks wait several times longer, its individual score does not establish a platform improvement. Those percentages are illustrative assumptions about evaluation, not measured results.

In an implementation, I would put total API budgets, concurrency limits, and retry allowances in the tool-execution layer, not just ask for restraint in a prompt. Budgets should cover the whole task and actual call chain. Spawning a child Agent must not issue a fresh allowance. Exhaustion should return an explicit limit or backoff state so the planner does not treat it as an ordinary failure and keep trying.

Recovery needs external constraints as well. When a service comes back, limiting admission and then increasing concurrency based on error rates and queues is easier to observe and control than releasing all accumulated work at once. Acceptance criteria should include aggregate completions, tail latency, interference with other tasks, and recovery time. These are design suggestions derived for software, not copied grid-protection rules.

After reading this investigation, I would add a scenario to an architecture review: assume every component correctly executes its own protective strategy; what happens next? Tests that prove each facility keeps power, each client retries, or each Agent works harder may still miss the costs of their combined actions.

For Northern Virginia, fuller investigation and model validation are still needed to assign the impact of different configurations. The public record already supports bringing post-transfer grid behavior and load recovery inside the reliability-review boundary. A sweeping "local optimum" explanation cannot replace that specific work.
